Why Is a Hermitian Product Defined That Way?

What

A Hermitian inner product is a generalization of an inner product for complex vector spaces.

Like a normal inner product, it’s a map:

\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to V

It’s \mathbb{R}-linear on each argument.

\begin{gathered} \langle u + v, w \rangle = \langle u, w \rangle + \langle v, w \rangle \\ \langle u, v + w \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle + \langle u, w \rangle \\ \langle x u, v \rangle = x \langle u, v \rangle = \langle u, x v \rangle \quad \text{for } x \in \mathbb{R} \end{gathered}

And positive definite.

\langle u, u \rangle > 0 \quad \text{for } u \neq 0

But then the definition gets weird. Instead of being symmetric, it’s conjugate-symmetric, and only only \mathbb{C}-linear on the first argument.

\begin{gathered} \langle z u, v \rangle = z \langle u, v \rangle \quad \text{for } z \in \mathbb{C} \\ \langle u, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, u \rangle} \end{gathered}

Usually, the only justification you’ll get when this is introduced is to say that it has to be this way for the norm \|v\|^2 = \langle v, v \rangle to be real, because if it were linear in each argument, you could multiply v by e^{i\theta/2} and get any complex phase you want on \|v\|^2.

This is true, but it still seems weird to me. They say you’ll get used to it. I got used to it, but for a long time, I still wished for some better justification than that.

Why

All a Hermitian inner product is, is a real inner product, which is compatible with a complex structure.

What do I mean by that?

A complex vector space can be thought of as a real vector V space with one additional bit of structure: a map J : V \to V such that J^2 = -1. Then multiplication by a complex scalar is defined as:

(a + bi)v = av + b Jv

So, let’s say we have a real vector space V and we have two additional bits of structure: A complex structure J, and a inner product (\cdot, \cdot). The least you could ask for to say these two structures are “compatible” is that J doesn’t change the norm of a vector. \|Jv\| = \|v\|

Well, if you have that, you have a Hermitian product, you just didn’t know it yet.

Proposition 1 (Ju, Jv) = (u, v) for any vectors u, v

Proof. \begin{align*} \|u + v\|^2 & = (u + v, u + v) = \|u\|^2 + 2(u,v) + \|v\|^2 \\ = \|J(u + v)\|^2 & = (Ju + Jv, Ju + Jv) = \|Ju\|^2 + 2(Ju,Jv) + \|Jv\|^2 \\ &= \|u\|^2 + 2(Ju,Jv) + \|v\|^2 \end{align*}

Proposition 2 (u, Jv) = -(Ju, v) for any vectors u, v

Proof. (u, Jv) = (Ju, JJv) = (Ju, -v) = -(Ju, v)

Proposition 3 \|zu\| = |z| \|u\| for any z \in \mathbb{C}

Proof. Let z = a + bi for real a, b \begin{align*} \|zu\|^2 = (au + bJu, au + bJu) &= a^2\|u\|^2 + b^2\|u\|^2 + (au,bJu) + (bJu, au) \\ &= a^2\|u\|^2 + b^2\|u\|^2 + \cancel{ab(u,Ju)} + \cancel{ab(Ju, u)} \\ &= (a^2 + b^2) \|u\|^2 \\ &= |z|^2 \|u\|^2 \end{align*}

Great! Now it almost looks like we have everything we could have wanted. We have a norm on our vector space, it’s defined by a real-bilinear, positive-definite inner product.

The product itself isn’t complex-linear. It’s not even complex-valued. It’s real valued on all inputs in our complex vector space.

But why should it be complex valued? The point of this thing is to define a norm, right? And norms are real. And this norm responds to multiplication by a complex factor in exactly the way we expect. So maybe we don’t need the Hermitian product at all. Maybe this is a different, better way to generalize an inner product from real to complex spaces.

Let’s investigate the properties that this new kind of product has.

I wonder what angle u makes with Ju. Well, (u, Ju) = -(Ju, u) by Proposition 2, but (u, Ju) = (Ju, u) by symmetry. So the product is zero. They’re orthogonal. I guess that makes sense. After all, 1 and i are orthogonal to each other on the complex plane.

Still, it is a little bit odd. That’s not the way things work in the Hermitian world, where iu is parallel to u.

We know what arbitrary complex scalars do to the norm, but what do they do to the product in general?

On the right (u,(a+bi)v) = a(u,v) + b(u,iv)

On the left ((a + bi)u,v) = a(u,v) + b(iu,v) = a(u,v) - b(u,iv)

Interesting. So if we just know (u,v) and (u,iv), we can calculate what any complex scalar does on either side. It might make sense to keep track of both of these together. Let us define

H(u,v) = \begin{pmatrix} (u,v) \\ (u,iv) \end{pmatrix}

Now I suppose the natural question to ask, is what does a complex scalar do do H?

H((a+bi)u,v) = \begin{pmatrix} ((a+bi)u,v) \\ ((a+bi)u,iv) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a(u,v) - b(u,iv) \\ b(u,v) + a(u,iv) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} H(u,v)

H(v, u) = \begin{pmatrix} (v,u) \\ (v,iu) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} (u,v) \\ -(u,iv) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} H(u,v)

Oh.

H isn’t just two reals. It’s a complex number. We can redefine it like this.

H(u,v) = (u,v) + i(u,iv)

And now we have \begin{gather*} H(zu,v) = zH(u,v) \\ H(v,u) = \overline{H(u,v)} \end{gather*}

H is a Hermitian inner product.

H is a Hermitian inner product that defines the same norm as (\cdot,\cdot).

The imaginary part of H encodes what (u,v) does when you multiply u or v by a complex scalar.

(u,v) is just the real part of H(u,v).

They both contain the same information, just expressed in different ways.

They’re the same thing.
